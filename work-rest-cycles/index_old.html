Indoor Heat Stress: Work-Rest Cycles Explained

Synthesized from NIOSH Criteria Document (2016) Pub. No. 2016-106 and OSHA heat guidance — continuous-work thresholds confirmed from NIOSH equations and Table 5-1; intermediate work-rest lines (45/15, 30/30, 15/45) require direct reading from NIOSH Figures 8-1 and 8-2

Acclimatized worker
  • Has completed a heat acclimatization protocol
  • Has not been absent from heat work for a week or more
  • New workers: 7–14 day graduated ramp (≤20% of usual duration on day 1, +20% per day)
  • Returning workers with prior experience: 4-day ramp (50% → 60% → 80% → 100%)
  • Acclimatization can be regained in 2–3 days upon return to hot work
  • Benefits per NIOSH (2016): increased sweating efficiency, stabilized circulation, lower core temperature and heart rate at the same workload
  • Charts use: NIOSH Recommended Exposure Limits (REL) — Figure 8-2
Unacclimatized worker — select for:
  • New employees who have not yet completed the acclimatization ramp
  • Workers returning after a week or more away from heat exposure
  • Workers newly assigned to hot areas
  • Workers with conditions increasing heat illness risk: cardiovascular disease, obesity, certain medications, prior heat illness
  • Per NIOSH (2016): "lack of acclimatization has been shown to be a major factor associated with worker heat-related illness and death"
  • Charts use: NIOSH Recommended Alert Limits (RAL) — for unacclimatized workers (Figure 8-1), approximately 2–3°C (3–5°F) more protective than REL

⚠ "DANGER!" does not reflect a NIOSH ceiling limit. NIOSH (2016) explicitly removed the absolute ceiling limits present in the 1986 criteria: "Data and scientific evidence were not identified to continue to support the NIOSH ceiling limit recommendations." DANGER! in this tool indicates conditions where work-practice controls (work-rest scheduling) alone are unlikely to be sufficient. At these combinations of WBGT and workload, engineering controls (spot cooling, task mechanization), physiological monitoring, and/or cessation of work should be evaluated by a qualified safety professional. Individual workers may tolerate or be harmed at conditions below the DANGER! threshold depending on acclimatization, fitness, health status, and clothing.
Additional adjustment factors: Clothing other than summer work clothing requires adding to WBGT. Common adjustments: standard cotton coveralls +0°C; single-layer impermeable suits +10–11°C. Consult ACGIH TLV documentation or NIOSH criteria for full clothing correction factors. All tables assume standard clothing (cotton/polyester work wear).

Hydration guidance (consistent with OSHA/NIOSH): Workers should drink approximately 1 cup (8 oz / 240 mL) of cool water every 15–20 minutes during heat-exposed work — not waiting until thirsty. Avoid caffeine and alcohol. Electrolyte replacement is important for prolonged exposures (>2 hours) with heavy sweating.
Key limitation of heat index for occupational use: Heat index was developed for shade conditions with 5 mph wind. In indoor environments with: (a) direct radiant heat from equipment, ovens, or hot surfaces; (b) very high or very low air movement; or (c) PPE/impermeable clothing — heat index significantly underestimates actual heat stress. WBGT is the preferred occupational measure in these settings.

Methodology for Heat Index–Based Work-Rest Limits

NIOSH expresses both the Recommended Exposure Limit (REL, for acclimatized workers) and the Recommended Alert Limit (RAL, for unacclimatized workers) in Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) units — not Heat Index. The Temperature + Humidity and Heat Index tabs in this tool use Heat Index as a practical field substitute because it requires no specialized instrumentation and is accessible to workers and supervisors without industrial hygiene training. The following describes how the limits were derived for each.

Acclimatized worker limits (REL-derived):
Heat Index thresholds were established using OSHA's published Heat Index risk framework as the structural basis (Lower Risk / Moderate / High / Very High / Extreme), which maps onto the NIOSH REL risk continuum. Work-rest cycles at each Heat Index level were assigned by cross-referencing the approximate WBGT equivalent for a given Heat Index value against the NIOSH (2016) REL curves (Figures 8-1 and 8-2). The approximate WBGT-to-Heat Index relationship used is: at moderate humidity (~50% RH), WBGT ≈ 0.70 × Wet Bulb Temperature + 0.30 × Dry Bulb Temperature; and Heat Index ≈ Dry Bulb Temperature + a humidity adjustment. This yields a rough correspondence of approximately 1–2°C WBGT per 5°F Heat Index under moderate indoor conditions, though this relationship varies significantly with relative humidity and air movement.

Unacclimatized worker limits (RAL-derived):
No NIOSH-published table expresses the Recommended Alert Limit (RAL) in Heat Index units. The unacclimatized thresholds in this tool are approximated by shifting the acclimatized (REL-based) Heat Index breakpoints downward by approximately 8–10°F (4–5°C equivalent). This offset is derived from the difference between the NIOSH REL and RAL continuous-work equations at representative metabolic rates — for example, at moderate work (300 kcal/h), the REL continuous-work threshold is ≈27.5°C WBGT and the RAL is ≈24.0°C WBGT, a difference of approximately 3.5°C. Accounting for the approximate WBGT-to-HI conversion, this translates to roughly 8–10°F lower Heat Index thresholds for unacclimatized workers. These are approximations only and have not been validated against a published NIOSH source.

Limitations of Heat Index for occupational use:
Heat Index was developed for shade conditions with light air movement (~5 mph). It does not account for: (1) radiant heat from hot equipment, ovens, or surfaces — a major factor in kitchens, laundries, and foundries; (2) very low or very high air velocity; (3) PPE or impermeable clothing; or (4) individual metabolic rate. In any environment with significant radiant heat sources or unusual clothing, WBGT measurement is strongly preferred and the Heat Index tab values should not be relied upon for compliance or enforcement purposes. The WBGT tab values, derived directly from NIOSH (2016) equations and Table 5-1, are the most defensible basis for formal heat stress assessment.
⚠ The 2016 NIOSH update removed the 1986 ceiling limits. The 1986 criteria included absolute ceiling WBGT values above which no work was recommended. NIOSH (2016) explicitly removed these: "Data and scientific evidence were not identified to continue to support the NIOSH ceiling limit recommendations. Acclimatized workers live and work in temperatures above the ceiling limit without adverse health effects." The DANGER! designation in this tool therefore does not reflect a NIOSH-defined ceiling; it indicates conditions where the work-rest curves cannot provide protection using work-practice controls alone and where engineering controls, physiological monitoring, or cessation of work should be evaluated by a qualified safety professional.
NIOSH (2016) RAL and REL equations — The 2016 Criteria Document (p. 93) provides equations for the 60 min/h (continuous work) curves, where M = metabolic rate in Watts (1 kcal/h = 1.163 W):

RAL [°C WBGT] = 59.9 − 14.1 × log₁₀(M[W])   ← confirmed NIOSH equation
REL [°C WBGT] = 56.7 − 11.5 × log₁₀(M[W])   ← confirmed NIOSH equation

Non-continuous work-rest lines (TWA derivation) — Substituting the time-weighted average metabolic rate (M × T/60) yields:
REL_T = 56.7 − 11.5 × log₁₀(M × T/60) = REL_60 + 11.5 × log₁₀(60/T)
RAL_T = 59.9 − 14.1 × log₁₀(M × T/60) = RAL_60 + 14.1 × log₁₀(60/T)

Resulting offsets above the continuous-work baseline:
45 min/h: REL +1.4°C, RAL +1.8°C
30 min/h: REL +3.5°C, RAL +4.2°C
15 min/h: REL +6.9°C, RAL +8.5°C

The TWA approach assumes resting metabolic rate = 0, slightly overstating allowable WBGT at rest-heavy schedules. The equations produce thresholds consistent with the confirmed anchor points (REL at 300 kcal/h, 60 min/h = 27.5°C; RAL at 300 kcal/h, 60 min/h = 24.0°C) and with the visual scale of Figures 8-1 and 8-2. Verify against those figures for enforcement use.
Indoor-specific considerations:
• Tables assume indoor setting with no direct solar load. All WBGT values assume natural WBGT (NWBGTi) or indoor measurement.
• Radiant heat sources (ovens, machinery, steam lines, dryers) can dramatically elevate effective heat stress beyond what air temperature or heat index captures. WBGT measured on-site is the only reliable method in these environments.
• Air movement matters: fans can reduce heat stress significantly; still air in a hot room is more hazardous than the dry-bulb temperature alone suggests.
• PPE and clothing dramatically affect allowable exposure. Impermeable suits can require reducing thresholds by 10–11°C WBGT equivalent.

Intended Use and Limitations
This document is intended as a preliminary reference tool for occupational health education and policy development. It does not constitute a compliance standard or legal requirement. Users should:
• Verify intermediate work-rest line thresholds (45/15, 30/30, 15/45 WBGT values) against NIOSH (2016) Figures 8-1 and 8-2 before use in enforcement or published tools — continuous-work thresholds are confirmed from equations and Table 5-1 and do not require further verification
• Apply professional judgment accounting for site-specific conditions (radiant heat, clothing, workload variability, individual risk factors)
• Consult a certified industrial hygienist (CIH) for complex or high-hazard situations
• Check for updates to NIOSH criteria, OSHA standards, and state-specific requirements

Developed with support from GLC-OHS / UIC School of Public Health. For informational and educational use.
Select Industry / Sector

🟢 Light Work  ~150–200 kcal/hr (116–233 W)

Seated or standing tasks, light hand/arm work, occasional walking on level surfaces. Little sustained muscle effort required.

🟡 Moderate Work  ~200–350 kcal/hr (233–407 W)

Sustained walking, moderate lifting (up to ~20 kg / 44 lb), pushing or pulling loaded carts, repeated bending and reaching. Note: NIOSH examples treat ~200–300 kcal/h as moderate; ACGIH defines Moderate as 180–300 kcal/h.

🔴 Heavy Work  ~350–500 kcal/hr (407–581 W)

Sustained vigorous exertion — heavy lifting, carrying, shoveling, or pushing/pulling heavy loads. Arms, legs, and trunk all engaged continuously at high effort.

⛔ Very Heavy Work  >500 kcal/hr (>581 W)

Maximum sustained physical exertion with whole-body effort. Common in several indoor industries — not rare. Requires the greatest heat stress precautions at any elevated temperature.

At very heavy exertion, core body temperature rises rapidly. Even moderate heat exposure (WBGT above 26°C / 79°F) can pose serious risk for unacclimatized or medically vulnerable workers. Continuous work without scheduled rest should be avoided above approximately 25°C WBGT (77°F) per NIOSH Recommended Exposure Limit (REL).
Determining Activity Level in Practice
Question Guidance
A job involves different tasks throughout a shift. Which activity level applies? Use the highest activity level sustained for 30 continuous minutes or more. If the work alternates between light and heavy tasks in short bursts, moderate is usually appropriate. When in doubt, use the more protective (higher) category — this is especially important when advocating for a worker or assessing risk.
The physical demands of the job seem harder than the category description suggests. How can the actual metabolic rate be determined? Workers and their representatives have the right to request that metabolic rate be properly assessed. ISO 8996:2021 provides a systematic measurement and estimation approach. The 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities (Herrmann et al., 2024) offers an extensive task-level MET/kcal reference. Heart rate monitoring during actual work is the most practical field method and can be done by a worker, union rep, or industrial hygienist. If a job is being assessed at a lower activity level than the work actually involves, that directly affects how protective the recommended work-rest schedule is.
Does wearing PPE, a uniform, or other special clothing affect the heat stress risk? Yes — significantly. PPE does not change metabolic rate, but impermeable clothing severely limits sweat evaporation and increases physiological heat strain. Apply WBGT clothing correction factors (e.g., +10–11°C WBGT equivalent for fully impermeable suits). Workers in coveralls, chemical suits, or heavy protective gear face substantially greater heat illness risk at any given temperature than workers in standard clothing — and standard work-rest schedules based on WBGT alone will underestimate that risk.
What should happen when very heavy work is being done in high heat — and a work-rest schedule alone doesn't seem sufficient? At WBGT above ~26–27°C (79–81°F), very heavy continuous work is hazardous for all workers regardless of acclimatization status. Work-rest cycles are a control measure of last resort — engineering controls (spot cooling, task rotation, mechanization of the heaviest tasks) should be the first line of response. Workers and advocates can raise this under OSHA's General Duty Clause if engineering controls are feasible but not implemented. A certified industrial hygienist (CIH) can document the hazard and recommend controls.
How can a worker or union representative identify the correct activity level for a job — especially if they disagree with how management has categorized it? Start by comparing the job tasks to the sector examples and general descriptions on this tab. If the actual work involves more exertion than the assigned category suggests, document it — video of the tasks, heart rate data, or a written task description. Workers have the right under OSHA Section 8(a) to request an inspection, and unions can raise misclassification of physical demand in collective bargaining or grievance procedures. A worker center, occupational health clinic, or industrial hygienist can provide independent assessment.
